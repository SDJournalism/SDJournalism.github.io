The Touchline Trap

Fabian Hürzeler was appointed Brighton & Hove Albion head coach on 15 June 2024 at the age of 31 -- to this day the youngest permanent head coach in Premier League history, and the first ever born after the competition itself began. He arrived in Sussex having just guided St Pauli back into the Bundesliga, and by May 2026 had done enough to sign a new three-year contract to 2029 with Brighton on the cusp of a European finish, turning down interest from Bayer Leverkusen to stay. The clearest thread running through that rise is what has become known as the touchline trap.

Hunting in pairs, not zones

Rather than screening passing lanes from a fixed zonal shape, Hürzeler's Brighton match up man-for-man across the pitch, every outfield player assigned an opponent to follow and press. It's built to force the opposition backwards rather than sideways, and it demands a level of repeat-sprint fitness Hürzeler treats as non-negotiable in his squad.

The touchline as an extra defender

The wide trap is where it bites hardest. Brighton show an opponent down the line on purpose, then collapse three or four bodies around him at once -- using the touchline itself to remove one passing option before a single Brighton player has actually closed him down. Every slow pass or backwards ball is the trigger: the front line steps up, and the rest of the team squeezes with it to stay compact.

Built to be broken, on purpose

Brighton's build-up shape works on the same logic in reverse. Centre-backs split wide, a central midfielder drops between or beside them, and -- when the space invites it -- one of those centre-backs will simply step into midfield himself. Every rotation is designed to drag an opposition presser out of position and leave a free man in the pocket he's just vacated.

In the match: Brighton 2-0 Everton

The trap showed up cleanly at the Amex. An Everton centre-back was shown down the right touchline, three Brighton players closing the triangle around him inside four seconds, and the ball won back deep in Everton's own half. Brighton worked it forward for their second goal inside twelve seconds of the turnover -- the trap doing the work a tackle usually gets the credit for.

The numbers

The clearest evidence sits in the offside count. In the opening four matches of his first Premier League season, Brighton won more offsides against their opponents than any other side in the competition -- a back line squeezing up the pitch together rather than sitting deep, exactly the platform the touchline trap is built from.
