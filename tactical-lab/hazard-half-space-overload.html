The Half-Space Overload

Antonio Conte's switch to a back three in the autumn of 2016 is remembered for rescuing Chelsea's season, but its most lasting effect was on Eden Hazard. Freed from tracking back down the left touchline, the Belgian was handed a role with really only one job: hurt the opposition in the space between their right-back and their right-centre-back.

The switch

After a chastening 3-0 defeat at Arsenal in September 2016, Conte scrapped his back four and unveiled a 3-4-3 for the first time at Hull City, where goals from Willian and Diego Costa secured a 2-0 win (Sportskeeda, 2016). It was a low-key debut for a system that would go on to define the Premier League season -- and Hazard's career.

Freedom in the half-space

With Marcos Alonso and Victor Moses providing the width as wing-backs, Hazard and Willian were pushed inside as the two "inside forwards" either side of Diego Costa. Hazard, in particular, thrived on it: fewer defensive responsibilities meant greater freedom to drift infield, hold the ball in the half-space, and pick the moment to combine centrally with Costa.

The Alonso overlap

The mechanism only worked because of the runner outside him. As Hazard drifted narrow and held the ball just long enough to draw an opponent's attention, Alonso was already sprinting past him on the outside. It left the opposing right-back with an impossible choice -- press the ball and open the lane in behind, or hold the line and let Hazard turn and run at goal.

The numbers

The results speak for themselves: Chelsea won the 2016/17 title with 93 points, the division's highest total that season, and Hazard scored 16 goals while averaging 2.4 key passes per game -- form that earned him the PFA Players' Player of the Year award.
