The Blindside Curve

Alexander Isak's finishing looks effortless because most of the work happens before the ball ever reaches him -- in how he bends his run onto the blindside of a centre-back while somehow still managing to stay onside.

Bending onto the blindside

Rather than sprinting straight down the channel, Isak curves his run so he can see the ball and his marker at the same time. It's a small detail with a big effect: he stays onside for longer than a straight run would allow, and the curve itself disguises exactly which defender he's attacking until it's too late for them to adjust (Coaches' Voice, 2026).

The double movement

The first run is often a decoy. A burst in one direction drags the centre-back's attention with him, before a sharper second movement the other way is the one that actually wins him the yard of space he needs to receive or shoot (The Football Analyst, 2026). Combined with genuine composure in front of goal and the ability to finish cleanly off either foot, it's a mechanism that doesn't rely on beating a defender for pace -- it relies on beating them for a fraction of a second, every time.

Built to finish, either foot

That mechanism helped make him one of Europe's most sought-after strikers: 63 goals in 135 appearances for Newcastle, including 21 in the Premier League in 2023/24 alone, third in the division that season behind only Erling Haaland and Cole Palmer. It was form good enough to persuade Liverpool to pay a British-record £125m for him in September 2025 (Wikipedia, 2026).

An interrupted debut

What that fee hasn't seen much of yet is good luck. A challenge from Micky van de Ven in a December win over Tottenham left Isak with an ankle injury and a fractured fibula that needed surgery, restricting his first Liverpool season to 3 goals and 1 assist in 14 league appearances (Wikipedia, 2026). The movement that justified the fee barely got a proper run. A first return to St James' Park this August, at full fitness, is the real test of it.
