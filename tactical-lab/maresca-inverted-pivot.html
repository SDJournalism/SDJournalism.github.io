The Inverted Pivot

Enzo Maresca has been careful to draw a line between himself and the man he replaced. "I don't believe in copy and paste," he said on taking over at Manchester City in the summer of 2026. "I'm not here to copy someone, I'm here to do my job, to install my football, the way I see football." Look closely at what that football actually looks like, though, and the line is blurrier than he suggests.

Short connections, not long possession

Maresca's Chelsea were not the possession monsters their underlying numbers might suggest. Seven games into his first season at Stamford Bridge, Chelsea's average possession sat at 54.9% -- lower than any full Chelsea season in the previous nine years -- but they ranked fourth in the league for passes per minute of possession. The point was never to hold the ball for its own sake; it was to string together enough short, quick connections in the middle third to trigger the rotations behind them.

The inverted pivot

The mechanism behind those rotations is a full-back stepping into midfield. At Leicester and Chelsea alike, Maresca has used one full-back -- inverting into a double pivot alongside the holding midfielder -- while the centre-backs spread to form a back three underneath, and the goalkeeper adds a spare passing option only when the press demands it. It's the same broad shape Manchester City spent a decade being drilled in under Pep Guardiola, just built from a different starting point (Coaches' Voice, 2024).

A free 10, not a fixed winger

At Chelsea, the clearest beneficiary of that structure was Cole Palmer, redeployed from right winger into a central number 10 role despite scoring 22 league goals from the flank the season before. With a full-back inverted alongside the pivot, Palmer could drift into the right inside channel on his stronger left foot, shoot, combine, or drop deep to collect possession the moment a defence pushed up. After seven games under Maresca, only one player in the league had created more chances. Manchester City's equivalent -- whoever ends up occupying that free, roaming 10 role between the lines -- is arguably the single biggest tactical question of his early tenure.

Counter-press, then reset

Out of possession, Maresca's sides press hard but selectively. Chelsea ranked second in the league for challenge intensity in the minute after losing the ball, yet only 12th for PPDA (passes allowed per defensive action) across a whole passage of play -- a team that hunts the ball back immediately, but drops into a settled 4-4-2 block rather than chasing it if the first counter-press doesn't win it back.

Why City already speak his language

What makes Maresca's "no copy and paste" claim more nuanced than it sounds is who he's saying it to. City's dressing room has spent ten years inside a positional framework built on exactly the mechanism Maresca favours -- an inverted full-back forming a back three, double pivots rotating to create central overloads, wide players pinning the last line. Maresca was in that building for two of those years himself, first managing City's Elite Development Squad and then working as Guardiola's assistant during the 2022-23 Treble campaign. His task isn't teaching City an unfamiliar language; it's persuading a squad fluent in one dialect of positional football to adopt his own (City Xtra, 2026).

Building his own squad

Where Maresca is genuinely diverging from his predecessor is off the pitch. He has said he wants a larger squad than the lean group Guardiola preferred, and his first major signing reflected that: a club-record £116m move for Nottingham Forest's Elliot Anderson, brought in on Maresca's recommendation after the midfielder consulted friends who had played under the Italian at Chelsea. More midfield reinforcements are expected to follow before City's rebuild is complete. "I feel back home," Maresca said of his return to the Etihad. Whether that feeling survives contact with the size of the job is the story of his entire first season.
