The Rest Defence

Arsenal won the 2025/26 title conceding fewer goals than anyone else in the league -- not because they sat back, but because every attack is built with the ball's eventual return already accounted for.

Building attacks backwards

The idea, sometimes called a rest defence, is that Arsenal's control isn't judged by possession totals or the volume of chances they create alone. Full-backs and midfielders deliberately hold positions during an attack so the team can apply pressure the instant it loses the ball, rather than committing bodies forward and getting caught stretched when a move breaks down (You Are My Arsenal, 2026). Wide areas are used to funnel opponents toward pressure zones that have already been set, so the press feels less like chasing the game and more like a trap sprung on cue.

The double pivot

Martin Zubimendi's arrival gave Arteta a second body to pair with Declan Rice in front of the back four, deployed in a 4-2-3-1 in 13 matches last season rather than the usual 4-3-3. It's an extra layer of central cover that lets Arsenal progress the ball through midfield without leaving the space directly behind it open (The Football Analyst, 2026), and it gives Arteta a second look he can switch to depending on the opponent.

Numbers that back it up

26 goals conceded across the whole 2025/26 Premier League season, the lowest in the division, with 19 clean sheets in 37 matches and only 87 shots on target faced all season -- 35 fewer than Manchester City had to deal with over the same campaign (Premier League, 2026).

Winning it in the last twenty minutes

The structure is a fitness demand as much as a tactical one. Arsenal outran 35 of their 37 opponents across the season and finished with a second-half goal difference of plus 24, more than double Manchester City's tally over the same span -- proof the pressing traps were still springing shut when everyone else had started to tire.
